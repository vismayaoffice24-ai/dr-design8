Awareness & Preventive Nephrology

Kidney Health & Prevention Guide

Your kidneys are quiet heroes: filtering approximately 180 liters of blood every single day, regulating blood pressure, and producing hormones for red blood cells. Learn the warning signs, prevention rules, and essential tests to keep them healthy for life.

Know the Warning Signs

Kidneys can lose up to 90% of function before severe symptoms appear. Don't ignore these early clues:

  • Swelling (Edema): Puffy face in the morning, or shoes feeling tight due to swollen ankles and feet.
  • Urine Changes: Urinating more often at night (nocturia), frothy or foamy urine, or pink/cola-colored blood in urine.
  • Unexplained Exhaustion: Severe fatigue, difficulty concentrating, or dizziness resulting from renal anemia.
  • Metallic Taste & Poor Appetite: Food tasting strange, nausea, or a persistent aversion to meat and protein.
  • Persistent Itching: Dry, relentless skin itching caused by phosphorus buildup in the bloodstream.
  • Difficult Blood Pressure: Blood pressure that suddenly spikes or requires multiple tablets to control.

8 Golden Rules to Protect Your Kidneys

Daily lifestyle practices that shield your nephrons from premature damage:

  • Keep BP & Blood Sugar in Target: Monitor numbers regularly; high glucose and blood pressure are the #1 and #2 causes of renal failure.
  • Hydrate Wisely: Drink enough clean water to keep your urine pale straw-yellow (unless your doctor has prescribed fluid restrictions).
  • Slash Dietary Salt: Keep daily salt consumption under 5 grams (1 teaspoon) to relieve pressure on delicate filtration capillaries.
  • Beware of Painkillers (NSAIDs): Avoid taking pain medications like diclofenac, ibuprofen, or aceclofenac without medical advice.
  • Stay Physically Active: Aim for 30 minutes of moderate exercise daily to maintain healthy weight and cardiovascular circulation.
  • Do Not Smoke: Smoking slows blood flow to the kidneys and doubles the rate of renal function decline.
  • Avoid Unregulated Supplements: High-protein powders and unverified herbal mixtures can cause acute renal injury.
  • Get Screened Annually: If you are over 40, have diabetes, high BP, or a family history, test your kidneys yearly.

Essential Kidney Tests Explained

Simple, affordable laboratory tests that reveal the true health of your kidneys.

🩸

Serum Creatinine & eGFR

Creatinine is a muscle waste product cleared exclusively by the kidneys. Your estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR) calculates your percentage of kidney function based on your blood creatinine, age, and gender.

🧪

Spot Urine Albumin-to-Creatinine Ratio (uACR)

The most sensitive screen for kidney damage. Detects microscopic amounts of protein (albumin) leaking through kidney filters months or years before blood creatinine starts to rise.

📡

Ultrasound of Kidneys (USG KUB)

A completely safe, non-invasive scan that checks kidney sizes, cortical thickness, detects kidney stones, and rules out urinary flow blockages or enlarged prostate.

⚡

Serum Electrolytes (Na, K, HCO3)

Monitors critical mineral balances including potassium (vital for heart rhythm), sodium (fluid balance), and bicarbonate (acid-base buffering).

Frequently Asked Questions

Clear answers to common questions asked by patients at our clinic.

Does an elevated creatinine level always mean irreversible kidney failure?

No, not necessarily. An acute rise in creatinine can be triggered by reversible causes such as severe dehydration, a urinary tract infection, temporary heart failure, or the recent use of painkiller drugs (NSAIDs) or antibiotics. This is called Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), and kidney function frequently recovers completely once the underlying cause is promptly treated. We evaluate your baseline numbers, urine tests, and trends over time to determine whether the condition is acute or chronic.

Can chronic kidney disease (CKD) ever be cured or reversed?

While chronic structural nephron scarring generally cannot be undone, modern nephrology can very effectively halt or dramatically slow down the rate of deterioration. By strictly managing blood pressure, optimizing blood sugar, and prescribing kidney-protective medications (like SGLT2 inhibitors and ACEi/ARBs), many patients live active lives for decades without ever requiring dialysis. Early detection is the single most vital factor.

How much water should a person drink every day?

For healthy adults with normal kidney function, drinking 2 to 3 liters of water per day is generally recommended. A practical visual guide is checking your urine: it should be pale, light straw-colored. However, if you have established moderate-to-severe kidney disease, heart failure, or active leg swelling, excessive water intake can cause dangerous fluid overload in the lungs. In such cases, your exact daily fluid allowance must be set by Dr. Anil Kumar.

Why are common painkillers so harmful to kidney health?

Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, and aceclofenac work by blocking prostaglandins. In the kidneys, prostaglandins are essential for keeping renal blood vessels dilated. Taking NSAIDs constricts these vessels, sharply reducing kidney blood flow. Regular or high-dose use can cause sudden acute kidney injury, chronic interstitial nephritis, and permanent renal scarring. Paracetamol is generally safer for short-term pain relief, but always consult your doctor.

Who should get their kidneys screened every year?

Annual screening with a simple blood creatinine test and a urine protein (uACR) test is strongly recommended for: (1) Anyone with Type 1 or Type 2 Diabetes; (2) Anyone with high blood pressure; (3) Individuals with heart disease or stroke history; (4) People with a family history of kidney failure or polycystic kidney disease; (5) Individuals who have suffered recurrent kidney stones; (6) Anyone over 60 years of age.

Emergency Health Warning: If you develop sudden breathlessness, an inability to pass urine, extreme confusion, or continuous vomiting, these are clinical red flags of acute kidney decompensation. Seek immediate casualty hospital care or dial 108.
